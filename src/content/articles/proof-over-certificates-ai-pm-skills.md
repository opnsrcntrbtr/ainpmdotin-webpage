## What did the 2026 credentialing research find?

**Credentials help, but they do not produce career outcomes on their own.** That is the central finding of Accredible's 2026 State of Credentialing report, based on two spring 2026 surveys: 1,000 learners who completed a course, certification or continuing education programme, and 194 education and training leaders at organisations that issue credentials.

The figures most relevant to product managers:

| Finding | Figure | Year |
|---|---|---|
| Learners who want a clearer description of the skills they gained | 44% | 2026 |
| Issuers ranking "demonstrate program value" as a top priority | 22% → 44% | 2025 → 2026 |
| Learners who say issuers could make a credential's value easier to communicate | 79% | 2026 |
| Learners planning another credentialed course within two years | 85% | 2026 |
| HR and talent leaders who say a digital credential makes skills easier to verify | 75% | 2025 |
| Employers who say they actively look for digital credentials | 91% | 2025 |

Learners also said what they want next to a credential: career paths, job titles linked to their skills, companies that hire for those skills, and salary ranges.

> A caveat before using these numbers: Accredible sells credentialing software, so it has an interest in the answer. I could not independently verify the survey data, and some figures come from the 2025 edition. Treat them as directional.

## Why aren't certificates enough for AI product managers?

**Because AI product work is judged on outcomes that a certificate cannot show.** An AI PM's core skills are things like defining "good" as a test set, trading quality against cost and latency, and deciding when the product should refuse to answer. A course completion badge says you studied these ideas. It does not show you applied them to a messy, real problem.

The research points the same way from the issuer side. When the share of issuers prioritising proof of value doubles in a year, the market is saying that a credential's name is no longer enough; people want to see what it leads to. For AI roles, where tools change every few months, that gap is wider still.

This is not an argument against learning programmes. I hold an AI product management certificate from IIT Kharagpur (with upGrad) and a product management certificate from ISB, and they gave me structure. The argument is about **what you show next to them**.

## What counts as proof of skill for an AI PM?

**Proof is any public artefact that someone else can open and check in a few minutes.** In rough order of strength for AI product roles:

1. **A live demo** that a reviewer can try with their own questions.
2. **An evaluation log**: the golden set, the metrics, the floors and the run history. This is the AI PM's equivalent of a design portfolio. See [Evals are the new PRD](/articles/evals-are-the-new-prd/).
3. **Open code or data**, even if you did not write all of it, with your decisions documented.
4. **A case study that includes weak results.** Publishing a citation precision of 0.194 alongside a recall of 0.943 builds more trust than publishing only the good number.
5. **A verifiable credential** that names the specific skills it covers.
6. **Writing**: articles, a glossary or a preprint that shows you can explain trade-offs to others.

## How do you describe skills in language employers use?

**Map each skill to a framework that recruiters and hiring systems already use.** The 2026 report recommends exactly this for issuers, and in August 2026 Accredible added three external frameworks to its platform: **O*NET** (US Department of Labor), **ESCO** (European Commission) and **NACE's Career Readiness Competencies**.

You can apply the same idea to your own profile without any platform:

- Write the skill as one sentence: "Turn a product requirement into a graded test set with metric floors that block a release."
- Align it to an O*NET work activity, such as *Evaluating Information to Determine Compliance with Standards*, and a NACE competency, such as *Critical Thinking*.
- Link the evidence.

This also helps with AI search. When ChatGPT, Perplexity or Google's AI Overviews answer "who in India has built a RAG product with evaluations?", they favour pages that state skills plainly and link to verifiable sources.

## How do verifiable credentials fit in?

**Verifiable credentials make the certificate part of your evidence checkable in one click.** Open Badges 3.0 follows the W3C Verifiable Credentials standard: the issuer signs the credential, and anyone can confirm it is genuine and see which skills it covers. Accredible announced support for both standards in January 2026.

For an Indian PM, the practical steps are simple: claim the digital version of every credential you hold, add the verification links to your LinkedIn profile and portfolio, and prefer programmes that issue skill-tagged, verifiable credentials over PDF certificates.

## How do you build a proof-of-skill portfolio in five steps?

1. **Name five to eight skills** for the role you want, taken from real job descriptions at the companies you are targeting, including India's global capability centres.
2. **Write each as one employer-readable sentence.** Avoid adjectives like "strong" or "expert".
3. **Map each to a framework**, such as O*NET or NACE.
4. **Attach at least one public artefact per skill**, and publish weak results alongside strong ones.
5. **Date the page and review it every quarter.** Stale evidence is weak evidence.

I applied these steps to my own work. The result is the [AINPM.in skills matrix](/skills/): eight skills, each mapped to O*NET and NACE and linked to demos, code, eval logs, publications and credentials.

## What should recruiters and hiring managers take from this?

**Ask candidates for evidence per skill, not a list of courses.** A good AI PM interview question is "show me an evaluation set you owned and a release it blocked". Certificates tell you a candidate invested in learning; artefacts tell you what they can do with it.
